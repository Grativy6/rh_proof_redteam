# Testing the plane line at primes larger than the rank

**23 November 2026.** At fixed $\epsilon$, the family eventually uses primes much larger than its plane rank. This rules out the $p=r$ specialization in our [first determinant test](2026-11-23-plane-determinant.md) at late scales. There is a substantive difference between the determinant character itself and the nearby type obtained by adding a symmetric power. We prove a large-prime protection result for products of determinant powers and exhibit arbitrarily bad cancellation on the nearby exact type even when $p>24r$. One unit of weight relaxation again suffices to detect the vector.

## Which prime regime reaches the estimate

For the higher-rank prime branch the release sets $r=\lceil32/\epsilon+64\rceil+1$. For each fixed family, $J$ is fixed, $\epsilon>0$, and every candidate prime satisfies $p>x^{\epsilon/(5J)}$. Hence $p>24r$ eventually in this branch. This comparison concerns the plane rank $r$. The number of normal coordinates is a different parameter. ## Two low digits can target any required precision

Take $p>24r$, $r\ge2$, and $a=p-r+1$. In an $r\times r$ matrix $X$, give row one degree $a$ and every remaining row degree one. Requiring minimum coordinate weight at least one forces a polynomial to vanish on singular matrices. Indeed a change of basis over the fraction field puts a zero column on any matrix of rank less than $r$. The definition imposes the condition in a generic basis and it persists on specialization. The determinant is irreducible and the rank-$r-1$ matrices are dense in its zero set. The polynomials satisfying the condition are therefore exactly $\det(X)H(X_{1,\bullet})$ with $H$ homogeneous of degree $a-1$.

Take $F=\det(X)X_{11}^{a-1}$. In its pairing with any polynomial of this exact type only the coefficient of $X_{11}^{a-1}$ in $H$ contributes. The column degree vector of every other term differs. The coefficient is integral for an integral test, since multiplication by the primitive determinant preserves the minimum coefficient valuation.

On row two choose a normalized factorial weight $U$ for column one and the same weight $V$ for all other columns. Give the other entries weight one. Grouping the determinant's terms by the column used in row two gives
$$
 B(F,F)=(r-1)!(a-1)!\,[U+(a+r-2)V].
$$
In the first group row one cannot use column one, giving $(r-1)!$ terms of equal factorial weight. In each other group row one can use column one, whose factorial contributes a factor $a$, or one of $r-2$ other columns. [Factorial unit on a digit block](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab026552e089d7afcfeff08d2defb9c005/src/Formalism/SharpRestriction/GrowthBounds/DiagonalSupport/PickDefs.lean#L11-L29) · [Plane pairing](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab026552e089d7afcfeff08d2defb9c005/src/Formalism/SharpRestriction/GrowthBounds/Lemma5aAt.lean#L259-L264)

Now put $U=U_h(u)$ and $V=U_h(v)$ with $U_h(t)=\binom{p^h+t}{t}$, $0\le u,v<p^h$. Their ratios attain **every class in $1+p\mathbb Z_p$ modulo $p^{h+1}$**. To lift a pair of digits $u,v$, append digits $i,j$ at the right, obtaining $pu+i,pv+j$ at the next depth. At depth $h$, relative to depth $h-1$, the ratio changes by the factor $1+p^h(H_i-H_j)$ modulo $p^{h+1}$, where $H_i=\sum_{k=1}^i k^{-1}\bmod p$. This follows by separating the factors with index divisible by $p$ in the binomial product. Set $i=j=0$ for increment zero. For a desired nonzero increment $d$, choose $1\le i<p$ with $1/i=d$ in $\mathbb F_p$, and set $j=i-1$. This proves surjectivity inductively.

Since $-(a+r-2)=1-p$, we can make the bracket vanish to any finite precision, with $p,r,a$ all fixed. On the other hand the test $G=X_{21}\operatorname{cof}_{21}(X)X_{11}^{a-1}$ selects the permutations in the first group. Its pairing is $(r-1)!(a-1)!U$, a $p$-adic unit. It needs only minimum weight zero on the $X$ rows. The example also tolerates a common nontrivial power $P_1$ on row one with zero low digits. Replace the factorial ratio $a$ by $a\,\gamma(P_1,0,a)/\gamma(P_1,0,a-1)$. This is congruent to $a$ modulo $p$, so the same two-digit lifting works for the new target. The congruence follows by separating the last block of the two factorial products, leaving $\prod_{j=1}^{P_1-1}(1+P_1(a-1)/j)\equiv1$.

The full local checks with a positive link cutoff can be imposed here too. Put the rows of another square matrix $Z$ first and multiply the source and test by $\det(Z)^b$, with $b=p^m$. Use two chain sets, first the $Z$ rows and then all rows, always including the required zero-degree frame labels. The first exact threshold $b$ forces the factor $\det(Z)^b$. The full threshold and link threshold $b+1$ then impose precisely the previous condition on $X$. For the unit function off the tested degrees one may use the integral product extension from the [first test](2026-11-23-plane-determinant.md#the-factorial-weights-can-cancel-to-any-prescribed-precision). It agrees with the actual factorial ratios on all monomials read by the pairing.

For $p>24r$, one may take $c=4r^2(r+10)(m+2)+1$ and slack $r\,d_{\rm LL}+26rc$ at every chain position, with frame powers one. For $m\ge1$, the source formulas give $e_P\le r^2(m+2)$ and $\kappa\le3r^2(m+2)$. Thus $c$ meets the strict budget. At $p=79,r=3,m=8$ the relaxed link threshold $79^8+1-60c$ is positive. All $X$ factorials used above are units. The determinant norm on $Z$ and the normalization of $F$ then have the bounds calculated in the first test. A remainder within its permitted valuation cannot save an exact-type test once the digit precision exceeds $r^2(r+10)(m+2)$. The displayed cofactor meets the weaker numerical conclusion. This construction works in the required large-prime regime of the local statement. We have not shown that the global weight construction selects this vector and these digits together. ## Why scalar determinant characters behave differently

Here is a sufficient bound independent of degree. Let $L$ be the coefficient lattice of a fixed multidegree in any finite collection of independent square $r\times r$ matrices. Let $f$ be a diagonal unit function satisfying the release's binomial approximation requirement for both $f$ and $f^{-1}$. For $p>24r$ and $A$ a product of determinant powers on separate matrices,
$$
 v_p\bigl(\langle A,fA\rangle_{\rm Fock}\bigr)
 =v_p\bigl(\langle A,A\rangle_{\rm Fock}\bigr).
$$
The hypothesis allows $f$ to depend jointly on all the exponents. The constant 24 suffices for the following estimate. [Approximation at every precision](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab026552e089d7afcfeff08d2defb9c005/src/Formalism/SharpRestriction/GrowthBounds/Lemma5aAt.lean#L194-L204)

Write $a_\beta=\Delta^\beta f(0)$ for its multivariate forward differences. On fixed degrees the Newton expansion in $\prod_i\binom{\alpha_i}{\beta_i}$ is finite and exact. If $|\beta|>6k$, taking the corresponding difference of the degree-$6k$ approximation gives $a_\beta\in p^k O$. Also every nonconstant difference vanishes modulo the maximal ideal. To see this, reduce the precision-one approximations for $f$ and its inverse. Their product is one on the entire $\mathbb F_p$-grid. It has degree at most 12, less than $p$, so it equals one as a polynomial, by interpolation in each variable. Both factors are constants.

The operator with diagonal multiplier $\prod_i\binom{\alpha_i}{\beta_i}$ is $D_\beta=X^\beta\partial^{[\beta]}$, with divided derivatives. Average its conjugates under a separate complex unitary group $U(r)$ on the column coordinates of each matrix. This calculation has rational coefficients. For one factor and $d$ marked derivatives, a common denominator is
$$
 H(r,d)=\prod_{j=1}^r (j)_d,\qquad (j)_d=j(j+1)\cdots(j+d-1).
$$
Indeed conjugation expands into integral combinations of lattice-preserving operators $X^\eta\partial^{[\delta]}$ with coefficients of degree $d$ in the unitary matrix and $d$ in its conjugate. The divided-power multinomial formula introduces no denominators here. Integrate matrix columns in reverse order. Conditional on the earlier columns, the next is uniform on the unit sphere of their orthogonal complement of dimension $j$. A moment with $h$ entries and $h$ conjugates is a sum over permutations of products of entries of the complementary projection, divided by $(j)_h$. Unbalanced moments vanish. This sphere formula follows by writing a standard complex Gaussian as its independent radius times its direction. Gaussian monomial moments are factorials and the $2h$th radial moment is $(j)_h$. Substituting the complement projection $I-\sum u_l u_l^*$ leaves at most $d$ pairs of entries for the subsequent integrations. Thus every denominator at the dimension-$j$ stage divides $(j)_d$. For separate matrices multiply these bounds, with the orders $d_i$ summing to $|\beta|$. [Related Haar integration formulas of Collins and Śniady](https://arxiv.org/abs/math-ph/0402073)

If $d<p-r+1$, $H(r,d)$ is a $p$-adic unit. Otherwise, by the factorial valuation formula,
$$
 v_p(H(r,d))\le r(d+r-1)/(p-1)\le d/23 \qquad(p>24r).
$$
Let $t$ be the valuation of the product of the denominators for a term. When $t>0$, $|\beta|\ge p-r+1$ and $|\beta|>6(t+1)$. Thus $a_\beta$ cancels that denominator with a factor of $p$ to spare. When $t=0$ and $\beta\ne0$, the vanishing of the difference in the residue field suffices. The averaged Newton operator on $L$ is consequently congruent to $f(0)I$ modulo the maximal ideal.

The line of $A$ is the whole relative-invariant space of its determinant characters on the square matrices at the fixed degrees. This follows by evaluation on invertible matrices. The averaged operator acts on it by a scalar, which is a unit since $A$ has a unit coefficient. Its pairing on this line equals the original pairing. Complex averaging is unitary for the Hermitian monomial factorial form, and the determinant characters have absolute value one. Applying this calculation individually to the rational operators $D_\beta$ and the real-coefficient polynomial $A$ gives identities for the rational bilinear pairing. They extend to the coefficient field before multiplying by $a_\beta$. This proves the equality of valuations. In particular the pure determinant family cannot by itself supply the late-prime failure. The nearby non-scalar example above avoids this conclusion.

## Unresolved

The prime comparison closes the original $p=r$ route at late scales in the higher-rank plane argument. The non-scalar failure persists for arbitrary $p>24r$. The released estimate explicitly permits the detecting cofactor. Testing a failure of that estimate still requires a source for which *all* its admissible tests lose too much precision.
