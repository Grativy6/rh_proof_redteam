# The centroid that hides four character readings

**25 November 2026.** Christopher Daniel Pang’s PAL and APCI question asks which distinctions one observation can certify. His May paper on shadow lattices prompted the comparison between separate readings and a shared reading at the same precision. Hearthline derived the finite-precision bounds and the four-character classification below. The Alpöge–Claude release supplies the local characters and the symplectic input being audited. The covering threshold for finite vector spaces is classical. [Pang’s shadow-lattice paper](../corpus/Shadow_Lattice_and_Dispersal_Lattice.pdf) · [PAL atlas](../corpus/PAL_v2.4-M_Mathematical_Realization_Atlas.docx) · [APCI](../corpus/APCI_Declared_Interface_v1.0.0.docx) · [Khare on subspace covers](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.2746)

At characteristic five there is exactly one way four pairwise distinguishable character readings can fail to separate on every common input at a fixed precision, after removing their shared character and invisible inputs. The four weights form a triangle and its centroid in the affine plane over $\mathbf F_5$. At any given input at least two readings coincide. The actual rank-four symplectic flag in the release satisfies an additional product identity which excludes this configuration.

## What the released argument needs

In ranks two and three the release chooses local characters on an inertia subgroup inside the cyclotomic kernel. Their residue characters agree there. Distinctness of each pair on the whole power-series ring is supplied by the choice of a point outside the coincidence ideals. Given a finite-index subgroup, the argument first takes a power of each witness to put it inside that subgroup. It then requests one element on which every ratio has infinite order. At a rank-four point the individual witnesses can initially lie in different pairwise residual kernels. The conclusion used there is again over full power series. ## How many coefficients suffice?

Let $k$ be finite of odd characteristic $p$, $A=k[[t]]$, and $\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_n:H\to A^\times$ be characters. A reading at precision $M$ is a value modulo $t^M$. On the common residual kernel all ratios lie in $1+tA$. Assume each pair can be distinguished there at precision $r+1$, with $r\ge1$. If the original witnesses have equal residues only for their respective pairs, raise each to $|k|-1$. This puts it in the common residual kernel and preserves its first nonzero difference. Indeed for $u\in 1+tA$, $u\ne1$, and nonzero integer $a$,
$$
 v_t(u^a-1)=p^{v_p(a)}v_t(u-1).
$$
The prime-to-$p$ part multiplies the leading coefficient by a unit and taking a $p$th power sends each term to its $p$th power. In particular a distinguished pair stays distinct at every positive power over $A$.

| Number of characters | Characteristic | Sharp worst-case precision for one common input |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 or 3 | Every odd $p$ | $r+1$ |
| 4 | $p\ge7$ | $r+1$ |
| 4 | $p=3$ or $5$ | $pr+1$ |
| 4 with $\chi_1\chi_4=\chi_2\chi_3$ | $p=5$ | $r+1$ |

Here $r+1$ can be taken as the largest of the individual first distinguishing precisions. For the rows with no extra loss it is then also the first common precision.

**Proof.** Form the joint image of the ratios to one reference character at precision $r+1$. It is a finite abelian $p$-group $B$. Each collision condition is a proper subgroup of index at least $p$. At most $p$ such subgroups cannot cover $B$. Each has at most $|B|/p$ elements and they all contain the identity. There are at most $\binom n2$ conditions. For a paired quadruple there are at most four because opposite ratios coincide up to inversion. This proves the first, second and last rows.

For four characters at 3 or 5, choose one witness $g_j$ per pair, $1\le j\le6$. Test products $g_1^{a_1}\cdots g_6^{a_6}$ with $0\le a_j\le6$. Fix all coordinates except $a_j$. Two bad choices for the $j$th pair at precision $pr+1$ would make a nonzero power of its ratio on $g_j$ equal to 1 at that precision. The exponent difference is at most 6 and its $p$-valuation is at most one. The displayed formula rules this out. Each pair therefore spoils at most $7^5$ out of $7^6$ inputs. At least one works. The examples below attain the bound.

## Why the centroid is the entire obstruction at five

Fix any precision at which all six pairs are individually distinguishable. Divide the four characters by the first. Let $B$ be the joint image of the three resulting ratios on the common residual kernel. Suppose the six collision kernels cover $B$. Enlarge each kernel to an index-five subgroup. There must be six distinct ones, since five cannot cover. In $B/5B$ they are hyperplanes.

Fix the first hyperplane. Outside it, each of the other five has exactly $4|B|/25$ elements when counted upstairs in $B$. These sizes sum to the size of the complement. Their outside portions must be disjoint. It follows that every pair of hyperplanes meets in the same codimension-two subspace. Indeed the intersection of two of the other hyperplanes is contained in the first and has that same codimension. Write $W$ for the common intersection in $B$. Each original kernel must contain all elements of its hyperplane outside $W$, since no other hyperplane covers them. These elements generate their hyperplane, so each kernel already was the full hyperplane. The joint image acts faithfully on the three ratios, giving $W=0$ and $B\simeq\mathbf F_5^2$.

Each pairwise difference now takes values in a group of order five. View the subgroup killed by 5 in the target as an $\mathbf F_5$-vector space. Each of the three nonconstant ratios is a rank-one linear map $u_i\alpha_i$ with $\alpha_i\in B^*$. Their kernels are distinct. For two of these maps the forms $\alpha_i,\alpha_j$ are independent. If $u_i,u_j$ were independent too, their difference would have rank two. Every difference has rank one, so the values of all three ratios lie on one common line. Choosing a generator identifies the four weights with points in $B^*=\mathbf F_5^2$.

Their six joining directions must be exactly the six directions of the plane. Normalize three vertices to $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$. They already supply slopes $0,\infty,-1$. For the fourth vertex $(a,b)$ the other slopes are $b/a,\ b/(a-1),\ (b-1)/a$. Requiring them to be $1,2,3$ in some order gives exactly
$$
 (2,2),\quad(3,4),\quad(4,3),\quad(4,4).
$$
This can be checked with $a,b\in\{2,3,4\}$ since horizontal and vertical joins are already used. Each possibility puts one of the four points at the centroid of the other three. Conversely, the centroid of the normalized triangle is $(2,2)$ since $1/3=2$ in $\mathbf F_5$. Its six directions exhaust the plane, so every observation identifies a pair. Affine changes and multiplication of every original character by a common character preserve the collision conditions. This proves the classification.

There are 2,000 such sets among the 12,650 four-point sets of $\mathbf F_5^2$. Each noncollinear triangle gives one set. Its centroid is unique, since two distinct centroids in the same set would have their difference killed by 4. Subtracting the collinear triples gives $\binom{25}{3}-30\binom53=2000$. ## Sharp examples and the arithmetic flag

Take $H=\mathbf Z_p^2$, $u=1+t^r$, and weights $(a,b)$ giving characters $(h,k)\mapsto u^{ah+bk}$. Exponentiation extends continuously from the integers since $u^{p^j}=1+t^{rp^j}$. At $p=5$ use weights $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,2)$. At precisions from $r+1$ through $5r$, readings distinguish the exponents only modulo 5. Every pair separates somewhere, and every common input retains a collision. At input $(1,2)$ the exponents $0,1,2,6$ are all distinct modulo 25. The first common precision is exactly $5r+1$. In the 625 residue pairs modulo 25 exactly 480 separate. The six difference kernels are lines of order 25 meeting pairwise only at zero. At $p=3$ the paired weights $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ give the sharp bound $3r+1$. Four distinct readings cannot fit among three first readings, and $(1,2)$ gives exponents $0,1,2,3$. [Binomial-coefficient replay](check_four_character_precision.py)

For the **actual rank-four consumer**, we can recover the extra pairing from its own matrix. Its local basis change is symplectic. The conjugated upper-triangular matrix $M$ satisfies $\operatorname{adj}(M)M=\nu I$, where the adjoint reverses both indices after transposing and applies the form’s signs. Its diagonal is reversed, with positive sign on every diagonal entry. Both factors are upper triangular, so the diagonal equation reads $d_0d_3=d_1d_2=\nu$. These $d_i$ are exactly the four local characters. The common twist applied when passing to the unitary frame multiplies both products by the same square. The equality survives passage to every finite precision. Its opposite pair differences repeat, whereas the centroid has six distinct directions. This independently excludes the characteristic-five obstruction on the marked input.
