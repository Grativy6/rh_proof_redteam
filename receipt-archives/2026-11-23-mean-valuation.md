# A zero valuation correction can hide an arbitrarily divisible conjugate

**23 November 2026.** One branch of the proof reads the constant term of a minimal polynomial, divides its valuation by the degree and by 12, then rounds toward zero. We found algebraic integers for which that reading is **zero at every finite place**, all the archimedean conjugates have a uniform absolute bound, and a chosen 17-adic conjugate has valuation **$12n$** for arbitrarily large $n$. There is no denominator in these examples. They meet the actual inputs to the lemma bounding the mean. We traced what the next argument needs from that mean. ## An integral family with no denominator cost

Let $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i)$ and let $\alpha$ be any complex root of
$$
 P_n(X)=X^{2n}+5X+5\cdot17^n,\qquad n\ge1 .
$$
This polynomial is Eisenstein at 5 over $\mathbb Q$ and at every place of $K$ above 5, since 5 is unramified in $K$. Thus its degree remains $2n$ over the coefficient field. Set $\beta=\alpha^{12}$. Every conjugate of $\alpha$ has absolute value less than 16. On $|x|\ge16$ the leading term dominates $5|x|+5\cdot17^n$, including at $n=1$. All the conjugates of $\beta$ are algebraic integers with absolute value below $16^{12}$.

At a place $w\mid17$, the reduction of $P_n$ has a simple root at zero because its derivative there is 5. Hensel's lemma gives one root in $K_w$ reducing to zero, and all other roots are units. Their product has valuation $n$, so the exceptional root has valuation exactly $n$. Raising to the twelfth power gives $12n$ on one conjugate of $\beta$ and zero on the remaining $2n-1$. The degree of $\beta$ is still $2n$. Otherwise each conjugate of $\beta$ in this list would occur with the same multiplicity greater than one, contrary to the unique positive valuation. This reasoning also gives the absolute degree over $\mathbb Q$.

The constant term of the minimal polynomial of $\beta$ has absolute value $(5\cdot17^n)^{12}$. Thus the normalized mean *before integer rounding* is
$$
 \frac{v_w(\mathrm{const})}{12\deg}
 =
 \begin{cases}
  1/2 & w\mid17,\\
  1/(2n) & w\mid5,\\
  0 & \text{elsewhere}.
 \end{cases}
$$
The release's correction is zero in all three cases. In the mean-height lemma the rational-prime denominator function can be identically zero and the same archimedean bound $A=(\log17+\log5)/2$ works for every $n$. The missing information is the location of a large valuation among the conjugates. For $d$ nonnegative valuations with mean $m$, the best bound on an individual entry using just these data is $dm$. This family attains it at 17 before rounding. ## Even fixing every archimedean absolute value

If denominators are allowed, there is a degree-two example with *exactly* unit archimedean absolute values. For odd $n>0$ take a root of $X^2-(2-17^{-n})X+1$. Its two complex roots are on the unit circle. Its discriminant has square class $1-4\cdot17^n$, which gives none of the quadratic subfields of $K$: $4\cdot17^n-1$ is $3\bmod4$ and $1\bmod3$, so it is neither a square nor three times a square. At 17 the two valuations are $n,-n$ by the constant product and the valuation of their sum. The mean is zero before rounding. Multiplication by $17^n$ is necessary and sufficient to make both roots integral at 17. This second example exposes the role of the denominator hypothesis; the first family needs no such allowance.

## What the proof actually transports

In the period calculation the variable $z$ being estimated belongs to the coefficient field $K$. The release uses an equality in which the new algebraic period value equals $z^{12}\beta$. If $p(X)$ is the monic minimal polynomial of a nonzero $\beta$ of degree $d$, the minimal polynomial of $z^{12}\beta$ is $z^{12d}p(X/z^{12})$ for $z\ne0$. The field generated by $\beta$ is unchanged, and the constant term gains precisely $12d\,v_w(z)$ in valuation. Consequently the mean divided by 12 gains **exactly $v_w(z)$**, independently of how uneven the individual valuations are. The rounding need not commute with translation across zero. The lower-bound proof uses the one-sided fact that an integer at most $x$ is also at most its rounding toward zero. The absolute-height bound separately pays for the two unrounded magnitudes.
